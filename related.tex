\chapter{Related Work} \label{ch:related}

\section{Vehicular Connectivity}
Network connectivity in vehicles has opened up large avenues for research. A large body of work~\cite{CVSurvey} has explored wireless technologies and standards (such as DSRC) for vehicle-to-vehicle and vehicle-to-infrastructure communication. Connected autonomous vehicles have also inspired proposals for cooperative perception~\cite{AVR, autocast, CarMap, EMP, vieye, shi2022vips, vimap}, and cooperative driving~\cite{dariani2019cooperative} in which autonomous vehicles share information with each other to improve safety and utilization. Unlike these approaches, \recap seeks to reconstruct driving scenes post-facto. Some have proposed approaches to offload route planning (but not trajectory planning) to the cloud~\cite{guanetti2018control}. Others explore  \textit{platooning}~\cite{schindler2018dynamic} in which vehicles collaboratively and dynamically form platoons to enable smooth traffic flows.

Beyond inter-vehicle collaboration, several proposals have explored infrastructure support for connected autonomous vehicles, with infrastructure augmenting perception~\cite{ravipati19:_vision_based_local_infras_enabl_auton,gopalswamy18:_infras_enabl_auton}, or delivering traffic light status~\cite{schindler2018dynamic}. Other work focuses on infrastructure-assisted traffic management at intersections~\cite{AIMSurvey}. \cip goes beyond this body of work by demonstrating the feasibility of decoupling both perception and planning from vehicular control.

\section{3D Alignment and Registration}
Iterative Closest Point (ICP) pioneered point cloud registration~\cite{besl1992method,trucco1999robust}. However, accurate ICP requires a good initial guess and sufficient overlap between point clouds~\cite{raposo2017using}. To address these, prior work has proposed RANSAC-based algorithms~\cite{aiger20084,mellado2014super,papazov2012rigid,trucco1999robust,zhang1992local}. However, these are known to be computationally heavy and tend to be less accurate compared to ICP. \recap ensures faster, more accurate, registration using overlap-scoped registration. \cite{liu2021coarse} scopes RANSAC to features in the overlapped region, but this is too slow (4-6~seconds per frame) for \recap. Most prior work on ICP focuses on accuracy~\cite{segal2009generalized}, registers point clouds from a single vehicle/robot~\cite{segal2009generalized,yuan2022gnss}, and uses ICP to reconstruct static parts of the environment. Instead, \recap uses ICP as a building block for 3D traffic reconstruction from multiple vehicles. To our knowledge, \recap is the first end-to-end system towards this. Recent work has explored deep-learning based registration~\cite{choy2020deep,gojcic2020learning,yang2020teaser,huang2021predator,fu2021robust,bauer2021reagent}, but these do not generalize well to our settings (\secref{s:recap_ablation-study}).

Recent work, VI-Eye~\cite{vieye} and VIPS~\cite{shi2022vips}, devises novel \textit{pairwise} registration between point clouds from a vehicle and a road-side LiDAR; in contrast, \recap performs \textit{multi-way} registration between point clouds from multiple vehicles. VI-Eye and VIPS extract and match features instead of raw 3D points which trades off accuracy for latency. For this reason, they require a large overlap without which registration would fail. \recap can register point clouds even if there is little or no overlap. Like \recap, VI-Eye and VIPS use registration accuracy and computation overhead as metrics. Unlike \recap, they do not consider coverage, an essential metric for our target application, reconstruction. More generally, because \recap is designed for offline reconstruction, it can operate in a different point in the trade-off space: it optimizes for accuracy by registering raw point clouds instead at the cost of higher latency.

\cip's alignment builds upon point cloud registration techniques~\cite{point_to_plane}. Prior work has matched features~\cite{persistent_feature_histograms}; these don't work well for \cip, where LiDARs capture the scene from very different perspectives.

\section{Perception, Tracking, and Motion Understanding}
\parab{Infrastructure LiDAR-based Perception.} Prior work has explored using infrastructure LiDAR to detect pedestrians~\cite{ped_veh_detection} and road features such as lanes and drivable surfaces~\cite{background_filtering,lanedetection}, and to warn vehicles of impending collisions~\cite{Aycard2011}. One work~\cite{lidar_point_registration_remote_sensing} proposes a genetic algorithm based LiDAR alignment, but unlike \cip, it has not explored the efficacy of an entire perception pipeline built on top of LiDAR fusion.

\parab{Deep Neural Networks for 3D Detection and Tracking.}
For vehicle-mounted LiDARs, prior work has developed expensive neural nets for point cloud based detection~\cite{VoxelNet,Yang_2019_ICCV} and tracking~\cite{Qi_2020_CVPR,fast_and_furious}.
These are for vehicle-mounted LiDAR and are computationally expensive; 
\cip exploits static LiDARs and can use more efficient algorithms, \secref{sec:cip_eval-microbenchmark}.

\parab{Motion Estimation.} Heading and speed can be estimated using DNNs~\cite{fast_and_furious}, SLAM~\cite{ORB-SLAM2}, or visual odometry~\cite{rosinol2020kimera}. \cip uses a lightweight technique since it relies on static LiDARs.

\section{Content Delivery and Immersive Media}
\parab{Mesh Streaming.} Techniques for compressing and streaming 3D meshes have a long history in the field of computer graphics. Early works focused on a single static mesh~\cite{hoppe1996progressive,kim2004view,el1999skip,rusinkiewicz2000qsplat}. Subsequent studies considered dynamic meshes, wherein the set of vertices and faces are fixed; only their positions change across frames~\cite{viola2023volumetric}. \mars considers Time Varying Meshes (TVMs), in which the number of vertices and faces vary over frames. These are common for 3D models captured and reconstructed from real-world scenes.

With the advancement of 3D sensors (\eg RGB-D cameras), numerous systems have been developed for streaming TVMs~\cite{jin2024meshreduce,orts2016holoportation,collet2015high,chang2019montage4d,stotko2019slamcast,zhou2023live4d}. In these systems, the viewers are typically indoor users (\eg humans using VR/AR headsets or mobile devices) in relatively stable network environments with limited movement. In contrast, \mars is designed for on-demand mesh videos streaming in outdoor environments, specifically for moving vehicles, where network conditions are more dynamic and prone to fluctuations.

\parab{On-demand 2D Video Streaming.} On-demand streaming of 2D videos typically relies on adaptive bitrate streaming (ABR) which adjusts video quality in real-time based on the viewer's network conditions. \mars similarly adapts the LOD level of mesh frames in response to drop in network bandwidth. But, it further optimizes the balance between rendering quality and network efficiency in two ways: (1) its choice of LOD is also guided by the viewer's position relative to the POI where the video content is overlaid, and (2) it culls frames based on the viewer's pose relative to the POI.

\parab{Augmented Reality in Vehicular Applications} has gained significant attention in recent years, driven by advancements in display technology, sensor fusion, and computational capabilities. Many works~\cite{kim2009simulated,abdi2015vehicle,rao2014ar,cao2018amateur,kim2013exploring} use the car's AR-HUD to enhance driver awareness by overlaying navigational hints, hazard warnings, and contextual information onto the real-world road view. Others~\cite{karanastasis2019novel,wiegand2019incarar} leverage AR for in-vehicle entertainment, but focus on rendering AR content based on 2D information and preloaded assets (\ie a simple texts or icons). \mars extends in-vehicle AR capabilities by delivering volumetric videos to AR-HUDs.
